What it is

Grief and trauma,
tangled together.

Traumatic grief happens when someone dies in a way that shocks the system: a sudden accident, violence, an overdose, a suicide, a death that doesn’t make sense. The mourning needs room, and so does the shock. People often feel stuck between the two, unable to grieve because the trauma keeps pulling them back to the worst moment.

It can look like intrusive images or thoughts about how they died, avoiding reminders, feeling numb or on edge, guilt about what you did or didn’t do, or a sense that life split into before and after. If any of that sounds familiar, you’re not broken. You’re having a normal response to something terrible.

Structured trauma work

I’m trained in Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT), an evidence-based approach for trauma. When it fits, we use it to work through the stuck points: the guilt, the what-ifs, the beliefs about yourself that the trauma left behind.

03

Understanding the loss

My foundation is psychodynamic therapy. Alongside the practical work, we explore what this loss means in the context of your life and relationships, so the grief can move instead of staying frozen.

How is traumatic grief different from regular grief?

In ordinary grief, the pain slowly changes shape over time. In traumatic grief, trauma symptoms, intrusive images, avoidance, hypervigilance, guilt, can freeze the grieving process in place. Therapy addresses both the trauma and the loss so mourning can actually move forward.

Do I have to talk about the details of what happened?

Not all at once, and not before you’re ready. We go at your pace. Part of my job is helping you approach the hard parts in a way that feels manageable, not overwhelming.
